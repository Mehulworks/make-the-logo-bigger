# Direction log

Directed by Mehul Varshney. The code in this repository was written by
[Claude](https://claude.ai) under that direction. The prompts are in [PROMPT.md](PROMPT.md):
the finished film as a single prompt, and the brief the first draft was built from.

This file is the other half of the story. A prompt gets you a first draft. Everything after
that came from watching each draft and deciding what to change, and this is the record of
those decisions.

## The starting brief

The first draft was built from one prompt, written after a round of research into what
actually changed in the designer's job between 1990 and now. It is the second prompt in
[PROMPT.md](PROMPT.md), under "Where it started".

It asked for a 45-second film in which one client sends the same request, "Can you make the
logo bigger?", in all six eras, while the whole frame restyles itself around it. That produced a
working 48-second film. Then the real work started.

## What changed, and why

### The running gag was wearing out

The first cut repeated "Can you make the logo bigger?" in all six eras. On paper it was the
whole concept. Watching it, the joke was spent by the third era, and there was a worse problem:
a viewer skimming a muted feed would read that one line six times and conclude the film had
nothing else to say.

So each era got its own request, and the logo line was cut back to the opening and the
punchline. A middle option was considered and rejected: keeping a small "…and the logo, bigger"
postscript on every era. It preserved the gag but added reading to scenes that are only a few
seconds long.

This change is also what made the film about something. Six identical requests say the client
never learns. Six different requests say the job kept growing.

### Accessibility became the spine, not a topic

The first cut showed tools changing. That is a trivia reel. Adding a second beat to every era —
somebody who cannot use what was just built, then a visible fix — turned a timeline into an
argument, and gave the ending its meaning.

Two rules kept this from turning preachy: the joke is always on the design or the tool, never
on the person, and every inclusion beat ends with a fix on screen, so it reads as craft rather
than a lecture. People are described by their situation ("on a bus, one hand free") rather than
as a category, which follows the persona spectrum from Microsoft's inclusive design work.

### No fictional brand name

The first cut named the client. A search turned up a real consulting studio with a similar
name, so the client became unnamed — on screen it is only ever "The client". Nothing is lost,
and there is no name that could collide with a real company.

### No future dates

An early ending jumped to 2027 for a final gag. That dates a portfolio piece within weeks and
claims something about a year that has not happened. The final era is labelled "Today ·
2023–now" for the same reason, and the callback now closes the last era instead of living in
its own scene.

### The ending: a lid, not a thrown laptop

The punchline needed the designer to react. A flipped laptop was funnier for one second and
wrong for the film: it reads as anger at the client, which is not a great note to end a
portfolio piece on, and it needs a visible person.

What shipped is quieter: a hesitation, a sigh, the laptop lid closing halfway and opening
again, then a resigned drag that grows the logo until it fills the frame and becomes the end
card. The joke survives; the tone stays warm.

### No characters, just a cursor

Drawing a designer and a client as characters was considered and dropped. Each era already
changes its entire visual language, so two characters would need redrawing six times, cute
mascots drift easily toward resembling existing ones, and once you draw a person you have
chosen what a designer looks like.

The cursor plays the designer instead, with one small reaction per era: a tapping foot waiting
for a 1990 progress bar, a sweat drop chasing the "Skip intro" button, a squint at a thumb
covering three links, a bounce when the contrast fix lands. Personality without a character.

### Pacing

The draft ran its captions close together. Every section was given room to be read — around
three and a half seconds minimum, longer where the text is longer — which pushed the film from
45 seconds to 110. Length was the right thing to spend here: a caption nobody finishes reading
is a caption that was not worth animating.

### Sound arrived last, and the film still works without it

The first draft was silent on purpose, because feed video plays muted and the captions had to
carry every joke on their own. The score was added only once that was true. It is one theme
re-orchestrated per era, so the music tells the same story as the picture: chiptune, synth band,
pumping pop, clean plucks, marimba groove, ambient pads.

## On facts

Every date and figure on screen was checked against a primary source before it was animated; the
list is in the [README](README.md). Four claims that had been taken on trust were checked
specifically because they were load-bearing for a joke:

- Photoshop 1.0 had no layers. True; layers arrived in 3.0, 1994.
- A screen reader announces the file name when an image has no text description. True, and the
  1998 joke depends on it.
- Table layouts used transparent spacer GIFs. True, though they were largely obsolete by 1998 —
  the joke sits at the very end of that habit.
- Thin grey text was a real trend, not a straw man. True; Nielsen Norman Group was writing about
  it by 2015.

The invented numbers are labelled as invented. The 40 / 26 / 2 counts in the AI era mirror
WebAIM's real 2026 failure rates but are not data, and nothing in the film presents them as such.

## What I would tell someone doing this

Ask for the hook and one era first. A whole film built from a cold prompt is expensive to
correct, and the first era is where you find out whether the tone is right.

Then watch the draft as a viewer rather than reading it as a spec. Almost every change above
came from noticing something while watching, not from a gap in the brief: the gag wearing thin,
a caption that needed another half second, an ending that was funny but mean.
